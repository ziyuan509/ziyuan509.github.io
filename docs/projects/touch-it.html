TOUCH IT!

Borrowing the layout of a kalimba, metal tines act as extended electrodes, so a tap or a swipe still registers while your hand stops covering the thing you are trying to read. No battery, no pairing, nothing to charge. This work later became a CHI 2026 workshop paper.

The fat-finger problem

A finger covering a key disturbs capacitive sensing, so the wrong letter goes in — bucked instead of bucket, because k and l are neighbours. Worse than the typo is what it hides: in a map or a document, the thing you most want to see is exactly the thing under your hand. Occlusion causes mis-triggering as well as blindness, because a blocked touch signal shifts and the system reads the wrong target.

Why a kalimba

Existing answers all add material between finger and glass — touchscreen gloves, styluses, conductive coatings, conductive patches — and all of them still put a fingertip-sized conductor on the surface. A kalimba is already held against a phone, with the far end of each tine resting on the screen. Treating those tines as extended contacts moves the hand off the display entirely, and varying their spacing and width turns a slide across them into a swipe.

Software, and the honest limit

The iOS prototype is written in Swift on UIKit: gesture recognition for tap and swipe, a cursor drawn where the signal lands, and a threshold that separates a tap from a drag. A stored previous position carries the cursor between gestures, so a swipe resumes where the last one ended rather than jumping. The limit is iOS itself — a genuinely global emulated pointer is only possible on a jailbroken device, so the prototype demonstrates the interaction inside its own app rather than across the system.
